GUIDE 01 · GROCERY SAVINGS

How to Calculate Unit Price and Save Money on Groceries

A shelf price tells you what a pack costs, but not how much product you receive for your money. Unit price solves that problem by converting different pack sizes into one comparable figure, such as cost per gram, litre or item. It is especially useful when a family pack, promotion and store-brand option all look different.

The unit price formula

Divide the pack price by the quantity in the pack: unit price = price ÷ quantity. A £4.50 bag containing 750 grams costs £0.006 per gram, or £0.60 per 100 grams. A £6.20 bag containing 1,200 grams costs about £0.00517 per gram, or roughly £0.52 per 100 grams. The second pack has the higher shelf price, but the lower unit price.

Compare packs in four steps

  1. Choose two products that are genuinely interchangeable.
  2. Convert both quantities to the same unit. Do not compare grams with kilograms until you convert one.
  3. Divide each price by its quantity, or enter both packs in the comparer.
  4. Select the lower result, then check whether you will use the larger pack before it expires.

When the cheapest unit price is not the best buy

A lower unit price can still waste money if the food spoils, the pack strains your budget or the quality is unsuitable. Promotions such as “three for two” only save money when you planned to buy that quantity. Compare drained weight for foods packed in liquid, sheet count for paper products and number of washes for detergent. For concentrated products, compare the usable servings rather than bottle size.

Keep a short list of normal unit prices for items you buy often. It makes temporary discounts easier to judge and helps you notice when a pack becomes smaller. Use the same currency and quantity unit throughout your comparison for an accurate result.

GUIDE 02 · SMART SHOPPING

What Is Shrinkflation? How to Spot It and Fight Back

Shrinkflation happens when a manufacturer reduces the quantity in a package while keeping the price similar, or raises the price while also making the package smaller. The change can be easy to miss because the box, bottle or branding may look almost identical. What matters is the price per unit, not the number printed on the shelf label.

How to measure the real price change

First calculate the old unit price and the new unit price. Then use: (new unit price ÷ old unit price − 1) × 100. Suppose a 500-gram product cost £3.00 and the replacement is 450 grams for £3.20. The old cost was £0.006 per gram; the new cost is about £0.00711. That is an effective increase of roughly 18.5%, even though the shelf price rose by only 6.7%.

Signs to check on the package

  • A familiar container feels lighter or has more empty space.
  • The number of portions, sheets, capsules or washes has fallen.
  • Words such as “new look” appear alongside a changed net weight.
  • A multi-pack contains fewer items, or each item is smaller.

Practical ways to respond

Read the quantity every time you buy a regular product and take a quick photo of the label if you want a record. Compare unit prices across brands and sizes rather than automatically choosing your usual pack. Store brands, refill packs or a competing product may offer better value, but check ingredients and quality as well as cost.

Buying in bulk can help only when the per-unit saving is real and you can store and use the product. If a smaller pack prevents waste, it may still be the sensible choice. The goal is not simply to avoid every reduced package; it is to see the effective price clearly and decide whether the product still earns a place in your basket.

GUIDE 03 · HEALTH BASICS

BMI Explained: What Your Body Mass Index Really Means

Body mass index, or BMI, is a screening measure that relates weight to height. It gives a quick numerical estimate that can help describe weight ranges across adult populations. It does not directly measure body fat, fitness or health, so the result should be treated as one piece of information rather than a diagnosis.

How BMI is calculated

For metric measurements, use: BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ height in metres squared. Someone who weighs 70 kilograms and is 1.75 metres tall has a BMI of about 22.9. Entering height as 175 centimetres requires converting it to 1.75 metres before squaring it. A calculator handles that conversion automatically.

Common adult screening ranges describe a BMI below 18.5 as below the standard range, 18.5 to 24.9 as within the standard range, 25 to 29.9 as above it, and 30 or more as well above it. These labels are broad categories, not personal medical conclusions.

What BMI cannot tell you

BMI cannot distinguish muscle from fat or show where fat is carried. A muscular athlete may have a higher result without having excess body fat. Age, pregnancy, body composition and some health conditions can also change how useful the number is. Adult thresholds are not designed for children and teenagers, whose growth is assessed differently.

How to use your result responsibly

  • Measure height and weight accurately, without shoes or heavy clothing.
  • Look at trends over time instead of reacting to one reading.
  • Consider other context, including waist measurement, activity, sleep and medical history.
  • Speak with a qualified health professional if the result concerns you or you are planning a major change to diet or exercise.

BMI is most useful as a starting point for a wider conversation. It can flag a range worth exploring, but it cannot explain the cause, identify an illness or prescribe a healthy target for a particular person.

GUIDE 04 · ONLINE SECURITY

How to Create a Strong Password That Hackers Can't Crack

No password is literally impossible to crack, but a long, random and unique password can make an attack impractical. The biggest risks are reused passwords, predictable patterns and credentials exposed by another service. A strong password works best as part of a wider security routine.

Prioritise length and randomness

Aim for at least 16 characters when a website allows it. Random characters created by a password generator are harder to guess than a name followed by a birth year, keyboard pattern or simple substitution such as replacing “a” with “@”. A long passphrase made from several unrelated words can also be strong when it is genuinely unpredictable.

Use a different password for every account

Reusing one good password turns a single data breach into a key for several accounts. Give every login its own password and store them in a reputable password manager. The manager can generate and fill complex credentials, leaving you to remember one strong master password. Never send passwords by email or messaging, and do not save them in an unprotected note.

Add another layer of protection

  • Turn on multi-factor authentication, preferably with an authenticator app or security key when available.
  • Keep recovery email addresses and phone numbers current.
  • Watch for phishing pages and check the real website address before signing in.
  • Change a password promptly if a service reports a breach or you entered it on a suspicious page.

A practical password checklist

Before using a password, ask whether it is long, random, unique and stored safely. Avoid personal details, common phrases and minor variations of an old password. Security questions should also use answers that cannot be found on social media; they can be stored in your password manager like any other credential.

Strong credentials reduce risk, while multi-factor authentication limits the damage if one is ever exposed.

GUIDE 05 · SALE SAVINGS

Sale Price Math: How Discounts and VAT Really Work

A “25% off” sign looks simple, but the final amount you pay depends on the order in which the discount and the tax are applied, and on whether the shelf price already includes VAT. In the UK and most of Europe, displayed consumer prices include VAT, while in parts of Canada and the US the tax is added at the till. Knowing which convention applies keeps the mental maths honest.

The basic discount formula

Multiply the original price by one minus the discount rate: sale price = price × (1 − discount ÷ 100). A £120 jacket at 25% off costs £120 × 0.75 = £90. For a second discount applied afterwards, such as an extra 10% at checkout, multiply again: £90 × 0.90 = £81. Two stacked discounts of 25% and 10% equal 32.5% off in total, not 35%.

Where VAT fits in

When the price already includes VAT, the discount usually applies to the VAT-inclusive figure, and the tax office simply receives a smaller slice of the lower price. When tax is added at the till, apply the discount first and then add the tax rate: final price = discounted price × (1 + tax ÷ 100). A $100 item at 20% off with 13% HST added at checkout costs $80 × 1.13 = $90.40.

Common sale-price traps

  • A discount calculated from an inflated “was” price that the item rarely sold at.
  • “Up to 50% off” where only a few items carry the headline discount.
  • Extra checkout fees or delivery charges that quietly erase the saving.
  • Comparing a discounted price in one currency with a regular price in another without converting.

Judge a deal in thirty seconds

Note the original price, apply the discount, then add any tax that is charged separately. Compare that final figure with the everyday price of the same item elsewhere rather than with the “was” price on the tag. A genuine deal survives that comparison; a marketing deal does not. Small differences compound across a wardrobe or a home refit, so the habit pays for itself.

GUIDE 06 · PERSONAL FINANCE

The True Cost of Subscriptions: Monthly vs Annual Plans

A £9.99 monthly payment feels harmless, but subscriptions quietly stack up. Streaming, music, cloud storage, fitness apps, news sites and software all drip-feed from the same bank account, and five or six of them can easily pass £700 a year. Annual plans usually cost less than paying month by month, but they only save money if you actually keep the service for the whole year.

The maths behind monthly versus annual

Start with the annual equivalent: yearly cost = monthly price × 12. A £9.99 monthly plan costs £119.88 a year. If the annual plan is £99.99, you save £19.89, or about 16.6%. A £14.99 monthly service is £179.88 a year, so an annual plan at £149.99 saves £29.89. The same comparison works in euros, dollars and Canadian dollars — the ratio is what matters, not the currency.

The break-even trap

Divide the annual price by the monthly price to find the break-even point. At £99.99 a year and £9.99 a month, break-even is 10 months: keep the service for the full year and the annual plan wins, but cancel after six months and you overpaid by roughly £40. Monthly billing is the cheaper choice for any service you might drop before the year is out, even though the sticker price looks higher.

Audit your subscriptions in ten minutes

  • List every recurring payment: streaming, music, cloud storage, gym apps, news and software.
  • Convert each one to an annual figure so they are comparable.
  • Ask three questions: did I use it in the last 30 days, would I buy it again at full price today, and is there a free alternative?
  • Cancel before renewal dates — set a calendar reminder a week ahead.
  • Share family or household plans where offered; they are often 20–30% cheaper per person.
  • Pause rather than cancel seasonal services such as sports streaming.

A £119.88-a-year plan abandoned after the novelty wears off is £119.88 of waste, while a £4.99-a-month app kept for four months costs only £19.96. Match the billing cycle to how you really use the service, not how you hope you will. Enter each subscription in the calculator to see its true yearly cost before the next renewal.
